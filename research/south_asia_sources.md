# South Asia sources and current map state, 1700–1947

An inventory for the planned South Asia pass, not a set of new atlas facts. No book behind a login was opened for this note. Plate titles, page numbers and URLs for Schwartzberg come from the public Digital South Asia Library (DSAL) catalogue and its IIIF metadata, consulted on 16 November 2026 (America/Regina). The current snapshot list comes from `sajjaddadashpour/chronoglobe` at `adc3ea0` (the merge of site PR #6).

Nothing here was invented to fill a gap. Where a date or a licence is not on a page I opened, I say so.

## (a) Schwartzberg, *A Historical Atlas of South Asia*

**Citation.** Joseph E. Schwartzberg, ed., *A Historical Atlas of South Asia*, second impression, with additional material (New York and Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1992). First published 1978. DSAL presents this second impression at <https://dsal.uchicago.edu/reference/schwartzberg/>. As of September 2026 the plates are shown with IIIF manifests and the Mirador viewer.

The 1992 impression is the edition to cite. The 1978 maps remain the core; the second impression adds front matter, addenda and corrigenda (text p. 263 in the DSAL table of contents).

The main **border plates** for 1700–1947 are in sections VII and VIII (territorial change, 1857 administration, imperial frontiers, the Bengal partition, and the 1947 administrative map). The accompanying essays start at text pages 210 (section VII) and 215 (section VIII). Other plates in those sections (elections, congress sessions, monuments, economy) are useful context but are not themselves border sources.

### How the page numbers and URLs were taken

Each plate below has a printed page, a DSAL map-group viewer URL, and the canvas id in the complete IIIF manifest (<https://dsal.uchicago.edu/reference/schwartzberg/manifests/schwartzberg_complete.json>). Printed pages were derived from the IIIF manifest path `/manifests/NNN/`, as page = NNN − 37. That reading matches the atlas’s own “p. 66” label in the DSAL table of contents for the Bengal partition plate, and it places the first Mughal plate at p. 44, which is where section VI begins.

The viewer URL form is:

`https://dsal.uchicago.edu/reference/schwartzberg/schwartzberg_mirador?` + the map-group manifest URL + `&canvasID=0`

## (b) Other scholarly sources for borders and rulers

None of the books below, except the public DSAL gazetteer pages, were opened for this inventory. They are the works I would go to first, with years taken from library or publisher records, not from memory of their maps.

### Imperial Gazetteer of India, 1909 (public domain; prefer this)

*The Imperial Gazetteer of India*, new edition, published under the authority of His Majesty’s Secretary of State for India in Council (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1908–1931; volume 1 is dated 1909). DSAL lists the editors as Sir William Stevenson Meyer, Sir Richard Burn, James Sutherland Cotton and Sir Herbert Hope Risley. The set is at <https://dsal.uchicago.edu/reference/gazetteer/>. The 1909 atlas (volume 26) is at <https://dsal.uchicago.edu/reference/gaz_atlas_1909/>.

Volumes 1–4 are the survey (*Descriptive*, *Historical*, *Economic*, *Administrative*). Volumes 5–24 are the alphabetical articles on provinces, districts and princely states, which is where rulers, capitals and state boundaries are described. Volume 2 (*Historical*) and volume 4 (*Administrative*, including the native states) are the first ones to read for this pass.

The 1908–1909 new edition is widely treated as public domain: it was published before 1930, and Internet Archive marks 1909 volumes with a Public Domain Mark 1.0. Wikimedia Commons says the same for the 1881, 1885–87 and 1908/09 editions, and warns that new matter in the 1931 atlas may still be in copyright in the United States. DSAL’s own gazetteer landing page does not print the words “public domain”; the site-wide maps policy still asks for permission before reproducing images. The underlying 1909 text and atlas are still the rights-clear source we can actually quote and, where needed, redraw from. Use the 1909 atlas, not the 1931 one, until that copyright point is checked.

This is a British official work. It is strong on 1909 administration and on the Company’s and the Raj’s view of earlier annexations. It is not independent testimony for eighteenth-century Maratha or Sikh borders. Where it disagrees with Schwartzberg or with a later scholarly history, keep both readings.

### Cambridge History of India, and the New Cambridge History of India

The original *Cambridge History of India* was published between 1922 and 1937. Cambridge University Press itself now describes it as overtaken by later research, but still useful for chronology and administrative structure. The volumes that cover this pass are:

- Volume IV, *The Mughul Period*, planned by Sir Wolseley Haig and edited by Sir Richard Burn (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1937).
- Volume V, *British India, 1497–1858*, edited by H. H. Dodwell (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1929). Also issued as a volume of *The Cambridge History of the British Empire*.
- Volume VI, *The Indian Empire, 1858–1918*, edited by H. H. Dodwell (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1932).

The *New Cambridge History of India* is the series to prefer for interpretation. Print dates below are the ones Cambridge University Press lists:

- John F. Richards, *The Mughal Empire*, NCHI I.5 (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 18 March 1993). Richards’s own end point for the imperial structure is 1720, which is directly useful for the first snapshots.
- C. A. Bayly, *Indian Society and the Making of the British Empire*, NCHI II.1 (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 31 March 1988).
- P. J. Marshall, *Bengal: The British Bridgehead. Eastern India 1740–1828*, NCHI II.2 (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 31 March 1988).
- J. S. Grewal, *The Sikhs of the Punjab*, NCHI II.3 (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 21 February 1991).
- Stewart Gordon, *The Marathas 1600–1818*, NCHI II.4 (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 16 September 1993).
- Thomas R. Metcalf, *Ideologies of the Raj*, NCHI III.4 (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2 March 1995).
- Barbara N. Ramusack, *The Indian Princes and their States*, NCHI III.6 (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 19 December 2003).

These volumes are in copyright. They would need a library loan or a licensed copy (the same way Chief of Staff opened Lane-Poole and Potts). They should not be copied into the repo.

## (c) Current state of the site snapshots over South Asia

Read from `public/data/world_*.geojson` on chronoglobe `main` at `adc3ea0`. Feature properties are `NAME`, `PARTOF`, `SUBJECTO` and `BORDERPRECISION`. There is **no `world_1750.geojson`**. The nearest existing years are 1740 and 1765. Other existing years in this span that were not in the original list, but that matter, include 1740, 1785, 1810, 1820, 1840, 1849, 1857, 1910 and 1947.

`data/polities.json` already has ids and year-spans for many of the states we need (Mughal, Maratha, Sikh misls and empire, Mysore, Hyderabad, Awadh, Bengal nawabs, Carnatic, Travancore, Cochin, Company rule, British Raj, Durrani, Sindh, Rajput states, Gwalior, Indore, Baroda, Nagpur, Bahawalpur, Jammu and Kashmir, and others). Those records are still Wikipedia-wired site cards. They are not yet atlas-quality sourced facts. Some snapshot `NAME` values also point at the wrong polity (see 1900 and 1945 below).

Precision is mostly `3` on the inherited historical-basemaps shapes, and `1` or `2` on some later edits. A higher number here is not a scholarly grade; it should not be read as confidence.

### 1700

Present: Mughal Empire, a generic **“Hindu kingdoms”** polygon, Bhutan, Tibet, Ceylon and Ceylon (Dutch), Kandy, Arakan (labelled “Indian princely state”), Ava, Shan states; a sliver of Safavid Iran.

Obvious errors and gaps: no Maratha polity, although the site’s own registry dates the Confederacy from 1674; “Hindu kingdoms” is not a historical state; Arakan (Mrauk U) was not an “Indian princely state”; no Portuguese Goa, no French India (Pondicherry and the other comptoirs), no Danish Tranquebar, and no English Madras / Bombay / Calcutta. The Mughal shape is a single late-Aurangzeb empire, which is the right *kind* of picture for 1700, but it has not been checked against Habib or Schwartzberg p. 46.

### 1715 and 1720

Maratha appears in 1715 (as “Maratha”) and 1720 (as “Maratha Confederacy”). 1720 also adds Kingdom of Mysore and Nawabs of Bengal, which is the right direction (the Bengal nawabs are usually dated from Murshid Quli Khan, 1717). Hyderabad is rightly still absent (the nizam’s state is 1724). Duplicates appear: two Ceylons, two Kandys, two “Hindu kingdoms”. One of the “Hindu kingdoms” polygons has `SUBJECTO=Netherlands`, which is a data error. The same Arakan mislabel and the same missing European enclaves continue. Hotaki Kandahar is present from the recent Iran redraw (precision 1).

### 1740 (no 1750 file)

Hyderabad, Awadh, Mysore, Nawabs of Bengal, Rajput states and a Maratha Confederacy are now named, and the Mughal Empire is still on the map, which is the right late-Mughal successor-state picture. There are still duplicate Ceylon, Kandy, Hindu kingdoms and Maratha shapes, and the Netherlands `SUBJECTO` error on “Hindu kingdoms”. Arakan is still labelled a princely state. Durrani is not yet present (the dynasty is 1747, so a 1740 map should not invent it).

### 1765

Much richer, and the first year that looks like a real regional map: Awadh, Hyderabad, Mysore, Maratha Confederacy (two overlapping features, one marked `PARTOF=Indian princely states`), Durrani Empire, Sikh Confederacy, Company rule in India, a second Company polygon named “India”, Madras, Goa, Carnatic, Assam, Sindh, Travancore, Cochin, Malabar, Nepal, Burma, Ceylon (Dutch), Kandy, plus small shapes (Ambur, Gooty). Afsharid Khorasan and Zand Iran appear from the Iran redraw.

Obvious errors: Mysore under Haidar Ali was not an “Indian princely state”; Travancore, Cochin and Malabar are marked `SUBJECTO=Netherlands`; Bhutan has `SUBJECTO=3` and `BORDERPRECISION=0`, which is a broken property; the duplicate Maratha and the two Company/“India” shapes need a source; Bengal nawabs have disappeared into Company rule (Plassey 1757 and Buxar 1764 make that defensible only if the remaining nawabi is treated as a puppet and is sourced as such, not silently deleted).

### 1783 and 1785

1785 is close to 1765 (including the same Netherlands subjects, Bhutan `SUBJECTO=3`, duplicate Marathas, and Sikh Confederacy). 1783 is a different, poorer map of almost the same moment: Hyderabad is missing and a “Golconda” appears (the Golconda Sultanate ended in 1687); Awadh is labelled “Oudh”; “Lahore” and “Multan” are marked as Indian princely states; “Mahratta states” and “Maratha Confederacy” both appear; the Sikh Confederacy name is gone. Year-to-year inconsistency this sharp is itself an error.

### 1800 and 1815

1800 has a “Sikhs” polygon (not yet named Sikh Empire), Nizam’s Dominions, Oudh, Mysore, Maratha Confederacy, Rajputs, Sindh, Carnatic, Travancore, Goa, Madras, Nepal, Afghanistan (the name “Durrani” has been dropped), and Ceylon still labelled Dutch. Travancore, Cochin and Malabar are still `SUBJECTO=Netherlands`.

The site registry itself dates Dutch Ceylon only to 1796, and the British occupation of the Dutch settlements began in that year (formal cession came later). A 1800 “Ceylon (Dutch)” label is therefore wrong on the site’s own terms.

**1815 is the worst of the mid-pass maps.** It collapses the interior to British East India Company, Maratha Confederacy, Mysore (now labelled an Indian princely state), Oudh, Travancore, Goa, Nepal, Bhutan, Assam, Arakan, Burma, Ceylon, Afghanistan and Sikkim. **The Sikh Empire is missing.** A point-in-polygon check of the Punjab box (about 71–78°E, 27.5–34°N) lands only on the Company and the Marathas, so those two shapes have been stretched over the Punjab. Hyderabad, Sindh and the Rajput states also disappear relative to 1810 and 1820. Neighbouring 1810 and 1820 *do* show a Sikh Empire, which makes 1815 a regression, not a historical change.

### 1880, 1900, 1914, 1920, 1930, 1938

The map becomes a single **British Raj** polygon (subject to the United Kingdom) with Nepal, Bhutan, Afghanistan, Ceylon, and a neighbour (Persia / Iran, sometimes Tibet). Princely states are mostly swallowed. Hyderabad, Mysore, Jammu and Kashmir and “Rajput states” flicker on in 1890 and 1910, then vanish again in 1880, 1900 and 1914–1938. Portuguese Goa is gone from 1880 onward, although Portuguese India continued until 1961. French India never appears in any of these years.

In 1900 (and 1910) a second polygon named **“India”** is a small sliver on the western edge (about 60.8–63.3°E, 25.6–29.8°N), not a second map of the subcontinent. The site registry maps the name “India” to the 1947 republic, so a click on that sliver in 1900 would be a false card. Burma is not a separate polygon in 1914–1938; if it is inside the Raj shape in 1938, that is after the 1937 separation of Burma from India and should be checked.

### 1945 (and a note on 1947)

**1945 is anachronistic.** The live map already shows India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka, and there is no British Raj. Partition was in 1947; the name Bangladesh belongs to 1971; Ceylon was still a British colony in 1945 and was renamed Sri Lanka only in 1972. This is the most misleading snapshot in the list.

1947 is better: India, Pakistan, East Bengal, Hyderabad, Jammu and Kashmir, Nepal, Bhutan, Burma, Sri Lanka. Hyderabad still independent in 1947 is defensible (annexed in 1948). East Bengal as a shape separate from Pakistan, and the name “Sri Lanka” in 1947, need a source. Many princely states that had not yet acceded (Junagadh is the obvious example) are missing. Schwartzberg p. 76 is the plate to use here.

## Proposed first set to redraw

Use existing snapshot years. Do not invent a 1750 file. Draw only from a consulted source, with an estimate label and a “scholarly redraw pending” note where we are still using a modern-province proxy, as on the Iran 1715–1785 work.

**First years:** 1700, 1765, 1800, 1815, 1840, 1945.

- **1700** — remove or replace “Hindu kingdoms”; add the Marathas if Schwartzberg p. 46 / Habib support them; add the European coastal settlements from p. 53 and the Gazetteer; correct the Arakan label.
- **1765** — first full successor-state map (Schwartzberg p. 54, 1707–1766). Fix the princely-state anachronisms, the Netherlands `SUBJECTO` errors, Bhutan’s broken properties, and the duplicate Maratha and Company shapes.
- **1800** and **1815** — Schwartzberg p. 55 (1766–1819). Put the Sikh polity back on the 1815 map; correct Dutch Ceylon; stop marking Travancore as a Dutch subject.
- **1840** — Schwartzberg p. 56 (1819–1857), with the Sikh Empire at its greatest extent before the Anglo-Sikh wars. Check 1849 next, once 1840 is stable.
- **1945** — undo the premature India / Pakistan / Bangladesh / Sri Lanka partition. This is the live-site error most likely to be repeated as a citation.

**First polities to get real shapes** (not a complete list of South Asian states): the residual Mughal empire; the Maratha Confederacy; Hyderabad; Awadh; the Bengal nawabs and then Company Bengal; Mysore; the Sikh Confederacy and the Sikh Empire; the Durrani empire; the Carnatic; Travancore and Cochin; Sindh; the major Rajput states, or a clearly labelled group if the plate does not yet support a split; Company / Raj territory that does **not** swallow the princely states; Nepal, Bhutan, and Ceylon under the correct sovereign; Portuguese Goa and French India.

A second wave can take 1740 (the missing 1750), 1783/1785 (to stop them contradicting each other), 1857 (Schwartzberg p. 60), 1900 (Raj plus princely states, and the stray “India” sliver), and 1947 (Schwartzberg p. 76).

I have not yet sat with the Schwartzberg plates to extract borders, and I have not borrowed the Cambridge or Habib volumes. That work should wait until schema v0.2 is locked and we can open the books properly.
